	- ## Module 3: Joining design Sprints
		- ### The UX Design Framework
			- The **design thinking framework** is a user-centered approach to problem-solving that includes activities like research, prototyping, and testing to help you understand who your user is, what their problems are, and what your design should include.
			- The design thinking framework involves the following phases: empathize, define, ideate, prototype, and test.
				- ![GG-UjxAJTjqvlI8QCc46CA_56b42a56f7534b9d80437b91462f53d8_UX_C1_M2_L2_R1_C-01.png](../assets/GG-UjxAJTjqvlI8QCc46CA_56b42a56f7534b9d80437b91462f53d8_UX_C1_M2_L2_R1_C-01_1790220476692_0.png)
				- **Empathize**: primary goal is to learn more about the user and their problems, wants, and needs, and the environment or context in which they’ll experience your design
				- **Define**: analyze your research findings from the empathize phase and determine which user problems are the most important ones to solve, and why
				- **Ideate**: he goal of ideation is to come up with as many design solutions as possible
				- **Prototype and Test**: the prototype phase, where your goal is to produce an early model of a product that demonstrates its functionality and can be used for testing. The test phase is critical to developing the right solution to address your user’s problem, and an organized approach to testing can help you create exceptional user experiences.
				- Key takeways:
					- Focus on the user.
					- Create solutions that address the user’s problems.
					- Collaborate with teammates across departments.
					- Validate your designs.
					- Iterate as needed to design the right user experience.
			- Though design thinking technically starts with empathizing, actual design projects typically start with business requirements. Business requirements set the boundaries → Design Thinking finds a user-centered solution within those boundaries.
			- **Empathize**
				- Some powerfull method, in order:
					- **Interviews** — questionnaires/surveys, in-person, phone, or video
					- **Empathy maps** — organize what users **say, think, do, and feel**
						- In a classic empathy map, designers draw directly from interviews to answer five questions about their users:
							- Who exactly **are** the users and what are their situations?
							- What do users **say** about their experiences with the product or similar products?
							- What do users **think** about their experiences?
							- What do users **do** before, during, and after their experiences?
							- What do users **feel** about their experiences?
					- **User personas** — fictional profiles representing groups of similar users
			- **Define**
				- Key tools:
					- **User stories** — describe **who** the user is, **what** they want to do, and **why**.
						- *As [user], I want to [action] so that [benefit].*
					- **User journeys** — map the user’s **experiences and steps** while trying to achieve a goal.
					- **Problem statements** — clearly define **who the user is, what they need, and why**.
				- Key idea:
					- **Empathize = understand users → Define = identify and clearly state their problems.**
			- **Ideate**
				- Key Ideation Techniques
					- | Technique | Description |
					  | How Might We (HMW) | Turn user problems in the define method into open-ended questions that encourage creative solutions. |
					  | Rapid Sketching (Crazy Eights) | Sketch eight different ideas in eight minutes, spending one minute on each sketch. |
					  | Competitive Audits | Analyze competitors’ products, strengths, and weaknesses to find inspiration and design opportunities. |
					  | SCAMPER | Generate ideas by Substituting, Combining, Adapting, Modifying, putting ideas to another use, Eliminating, and Rearranging. |
					  | Collaboration | Brainstorm with team members, users, and business stakeholders to generate diverse ideas. |
				- Key idea:
					- **Ideate = generate many possible solutions → evaluate them against user needs and business requirements → select solutions to prototype.**
			- **Prototype**
				- Key Concepts:
					- Information Architecture (IA): Organizes information for easy navigation.
						- Sitemap: Shows the hierarchy and sequence of pages.
							- ![Vr4ZWI44THiKSFkuDU1pmw_d16d76758f2647c1bdf06f04ae974cf1_OpcC9IPta-FpQNYdt8Mi-ye-ao8dDzbEk3aHoU9dwQcZVYDMuKvqR37yCjpYeYSU8fWUVhcZD-FHlvTpOM5MOCn6SX2NKYhQLHqKvRkofwgQVns-cQmeVcUlLFjTmgvoIbbQ-ii58slNoZNCtl-mQ0w.png](../assets/Vr4ZWI44THiKSFkuDU1pmw_d16d76758f2647c1bdf06f04ae974cf1_OpcC9IPta-FpQNYdt8Mi-ye-ao8dDzbEk3aHoU9dwQcZVYDMuKvqR37yCjpYeYSU8fWUVhcZD-FHlvTpOM5MOCn6SX2NKYhQLHqKvRkofwgQVns-cQmeVcUlLFjTmgvoIbbQ-ii58slNoZNCtl-mQ0w_1790305378202_0.png)
					- Wireframes: Rough layouts of a product.
						- Paper: Hand-drawn sketches.
							- ![OHKeW_jbSkyIjYX1yRDPbw_cf8743c8713d4ab9b1f2874e1ecc4af1_90ghDYN9KmUaD_r7gGY-QUE6JxWgSk2w_Dfk_S4eoS4O81P0t77T_BiKAgBz2vNPuUm8vTQbnMxS3yOH-h76KgVhUwsEbD9AH49dC9iES4SHkQNJauB6erjpaHU5wO7lc1BJ9S1VVMFGIbFkF9dE7TE.png](../assets/OHKeW_jbSkyIjYX1yRDPbw_cf8743c8713d4ab9b1f2874e1ecc4af1_90ghDYN9KmUaD_r7gGY-QUE6JxWgSk2w_Dfk_S4eoS4O81P0t77T_BiKAgBz2vNPuUm8vTQbnMxS3yOH-h76KgVhUwsEbD9AH49dC9iES4SHkQNJauB6erjpaHU5wO7lc1BJ9S1VVMFGIbFkF9dE7TE_1790305525133_0.png)
						- Digital: Created using tools like Figma.
					- Low-fidelity prototypes: Simple, basic, interactive models.
					- High-fidelity prototypes: Detailed models that closely resemble the final product.
						- ![TclqzHRPQgKfJi_YAqaBcQ_73f31f19bce24014b9cc001320b147f1_VQQd61OfSUoo9f06PyflGu0CMmpgcl_fV_Q-iLeVZTDI451gDmofuX7nQPRe-TBluipxPnQydXXKTKn5Olb9VsDFddgrMgznGXLyikTUuMlX0W-q5oiJBl0rjgbmmDJAOeNiXojMW5nMKq-tsN879Gw.png](../assets/TclqzHRPQgKfJi_YAqaBcQ_73f31f19bce24014b9cc001320b147f1_VQQd61OfSUoo9f06PyflGu0CMmpgcl_fV_Q-iLeVZTDI451gDmofuX7nQPRe-TBluipxPnQydXXKTKn5Olb9VsDFddgrMgznGXLyikTUuMlX0W-q5oiJBl0rjgbmmDJAOeNiXojMW5nMKq-tsN879Gw_1790305682807_0.png)
				- Key Takeaway:
					- Designers create early models of a product to visualize, test, and improve solutions before building the final product.
					- Prototypes help designers gather feedback, identify problems, and improve designs. Prototyping and testing work together in an iterative process.
			- **Testing**
				- The test phase is the fifth stage of design thinking. Designers test prototypes with users to gather feedback, identify problems, and improve the design.
				- Key Concepts
					- Who: Real users matching the user personas.
					- What: Test the prototype’s usability and functionality.
					- Where/When: In person or virtually.
					- Why: Validate design decisions and identify usability issues.
					- How: Observe users, conduct interviews, and collect feedback.
				- Key Takeaway
					- Testing helps designers improve prototypes through feedback and repeated iterations until the design meets user needs and business requirements. Then, developers turn the finalized design into the actual product.
		- ### Understand design sprints
			- A **design sprint** is a fast, structured way to solve a specific design problem through collaboration, prototyping, and testing.
			- Resources:
				- https://designsprintkit.withgoogle.com/
				- https://designsprintkit.withgoogle.com/case-studies
				- https://designsprintkit.withgoogle.com/resources/overview
			- Design Thinking vs. Design Sprint
				- | Design Thinking | Design Sprint |
				  | ---- | ---- | ---- |
				  | Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test | Understand → Ideate → Decide → Prototype → Test |
				  | Broad problem-solving framework | Specific process for a design challenge |
				  | Open-ended | Time-bound, usually **5 days** |
				  | Uses many methods and tools | Uses a defined process |
			- Key Takeaway
				- **Design thinking** is the overall approach, while a **design sprint** is a focused, time-limited method for applying similar principles to solve a specific problem quickly. UX designers contribute by researching, sketching, wireframing, prototyping, and testing.
		- ### Plan design sprints
			- Consist of: User Research, Call in the experts, Find the right space (has whiteboard, comfortable furnitures), Gather supplies (markers, sticky notes, snacks and water), Establish sprint rules, Plan introductions (Ice breaker), Post-sprint planning (documentation)
			- **Sprint brief**: A document that you share with all your attendees to help them prepare for the sprint
		- ### Participate in design sprints
		- ### Reflect on design sprints
			- A **retrospective** is a team meeting held after a design sprint to reflect on the **process** and improve future sprints.
			- 2 key questions:
				- 1. What Went Well?
					- Identify successful tools, methods, and teamwork.
					- Celebrate achievements and team contributions.
					- Apply successful practices to future sprints.
				- 2. What Can Be Improved?
					- Identify challenges and mistakes during each sprint phase.
					- Encourage honest feedback, including anonymous suggestions.
					- Avoid groupthink and blaming individuals.

- # 2. Start the UX Design Process: Empathize, Define, and Ideate
	- ## Module 1: Empathizing with users and defining pain points
		- ### Empathize with users
			- **Empathy** is the ability to understand another person's feelings, thoughts, and perspective.
			- | Practice | How to apply it |
			  | 1. Ask questions | Ask what, how, and why to understand users' needs and motivations. |
			  | 2. Observe users | Watch behavior, interactions, and nonverbal cues. Take notes or record sessions. |
			  | 3. Active listening | Focus on what users say without planning your response or interrupting. |
			  | 4. Request input | Collect unbiased feedback from diverse users using open-ended questions. |
			  | 5. Keep an open mind | Recognize personal biases and avoid imposing your own opinions on users. |
			  | 6. Stay current on UX research | Follow researchers and communities to understand evolving research and human psychology. |
			- **Determine Research Goals and Questions**
				- **Three steps to plan user interviews**
					- Before interviewing users, prepare:
					  |Step|Purpose|
					  | --- | --- |
					  |1. Research goals|Define what you want to learn and guide design decisions.|
					  |2. Participant characteristics|Identify target users and recruit a representative, diverse sample.|
					  |3. Interview questions|Prepare consistent questions to explore users' needs, behaviors, and pain points.|
				- **1. Define research goals**
					- Research goals should be clear, specific, and focused on the problem you're solving.
					- Ask:
						- Who are my users, and what problems do they face?
						- What do I need to learn from interviews?
						- Which user needs or pain points require further investigation?
					- Example: Zia's Pizza app
						- Understand challenges with busy schedules and meal planning.
						- Identify frustrations when ordering food from restaurants.
				- **2. Define participant characteristics**
					- Recruit participants who represent your product's target users while maintaining diversity.
					- Consider:
						- Age and location
						- Occupation and lifestyle
						- Gender
						- Disabilities and accessibility needs
						- Relevant behaviors (e.g., ordering food weekly)
					- Use business requirements, preliminary research, and stakeholder input to identify end users.
				- **3. Write effective interview questions**
					- Effective questions have five characteristics:
					  |Characteristic|Meaning|
					  | --- | --- |
					  |Relevant|Related to the research goals.|
					  |Open-ended|Encourage detailed answers, not just yes/no.|
					  |Clear|Short, simple, and easy to understand.|
					  |Neutral|Avoid assumptions or leading participants toward an answer.|
					  |Follow-up friendly|Encourage elaboration and deeper exploration.|
					- Example:
						- Neutral and open-ended: "How do you balance your responsibilities with meal planning?" ✅
						- Leading question: "Do you order food because you don't have time to cook or because you don't want to cook?" ⛔
						- Use follow-up prompts such as "Tell me more about that" and "Why?" to explore users' experiences. ✅
				- Key takeaway
					- Research goals → Participant characteristics → Interview questions → User insights → Design decisions
					- Prepare thoughtful interview plans, keep questions aligned with research goals, and adapt them as the project evolves.
			- **Interview questions**
				- U can do it in sequence:
					- **Context → Behavior → Thoughts → Feelings → Goals → Needs → Pain points → Workarounds**
				- Example of core questions:
					- **Warm-up questions (build comfort)**
						- Can you tell me a little about yourself and your daily routine?
						- How often do you shop or order things online?
						- What device do you usually use for that: phone, laptop, or tablet?
					- **Past experience (the most valuable ones)**
						- Tell me about the last time you ordered a cake (or product/service). How did it go?
						- Walk me through the steps you took, from start to finish.
						- What made you choose that place over others?
						- Was there any moment where you got confused or stuck?
					- **Pain points and frustrations**
						- What's the most annoying part of ordering online?
						- Have you ever given up halfway through ordering? What happened?
						- What do you wish was easier?
					- **Feelings and motivations**
						- How did you feel while waiting for your order?
						- What makes you trust a website enough to pay on it?
						- What would make you worried or hesitant to order?
					- **Habits and context**
						- When and where do you usually order? (at home, at work, late at night?)
						- Do you compare several websites before deciding? How?
						- What information do you look for first on a product page?
					- **Closing questions**
						- If you could change one thing about ordering online, what would it be?
						- Is there anything else you'd like to share that I didn't ask about?
					- **Tips for asking good questions**
						- **Ask open-ended questions.** "How did you find the checkout?" is better than "Was the checkout easy?", because yes/no questions give you little to work with.
						- **Ask about real past experiences, not the future.** "What did you do last time?" gives more honest answers than "Would you use this feature?"
						- **Don't lead the user.** Avoid questions like "Don't you think the prices should be bigger?" since they push your own idea.
						- **Follow up with "why?"** or "Can you tell me more about that?" to dig deeper.
						- **Listen more than you talk**, and note their emotions, not just their words.
		- ### Learn about empathy maps
			- **Empathy map** is a visual chart that organizes user interview findings into four categories to help UX designers understand users' needs, behaviors, and pain points.
			- **The four quadrants**
			  | Quadrant | What to capture |
			  | Says | Exact quotes from the user. Identify recurring problems and desired benefits. |
			  | Thinks | User's thoughts and reasonable inferences based on evidence. Avoid assumptions. |
			  | Does | Actions and behaviors the user takes to overcome challenges. |
			  | Feels | Expressed emotions or observed feelings, such as worry, frustration, or excitement. |
			- ![empathy map.png](../assets/empathy_map_1790695314776_0.png)
			- How to create an empathy map
				- Add the interviewee's name.
				- Record verbatim quotes in Says.
				- Summarize thoughts and evidence-based inferences in Thinks.
				- Document actions in Does.
				- Record emotions in Feels. Ask "How does this make you feel?" if needed.
			- **Types of empathy maps**
			  | Type | Purpose |
			  | One-user empathy map | Summarizes findings from one person's interview. |
			  | Aggregated empathy map | Combines multiple individual maps to identify common themes and user segments. |
			- Example: Dog-walking app
				- Makayla is a busy teacher who needs reliable dog-walking services.
				- Says: Wants to prescreen walkers and book them consistently.
				- Thinks: Needs experienced, trustworthy walkers.
				- Does: Walks her dogs morning and evening and pays her neighbor to help.
				- Feels: Guilty, sad, and worried about her dogs' care.
				- ![empathy map 2.png](../assets/empathy_map_2_1790695352040_0.png)
				- These findings help identify opportunities for the app, such as verified walkers and advance or recurring bookings.
			- **Key takeaway**:
				- Empathy maps turn interview data into organized user insights. Combining multiple maps helps identify shared user needs and guide design decisions.
		- ### Identify user pain points
			- **Four Categories of User Pain Points**
			  | Category | Meaning | Example |
			  | ---- | ---- | ---- |
			  | **Financial** | Problems related to money or cost | A paywall prevents users from accessing content |
			  | **Product** | Problems with the product's design, quality, or functionality | A confusing door design such as a **Norman Door** |
			  | **Process** | Problems that make it difficult to complete a task or move through a process | A frustrating online checkout process |
			  | **Support** | Problems finding or accessing help and information | Customer service information is difficult to find |
			- **Key takeaway**
				- **Pain points are frustrations that prevent users from achieving their goals.** UX designers use empathy and research—such as empathy maps—to uncover these problems and design solutions around users' actual needs rather than assumptions.
			- **Remember:** **Financial → Product → Process → Support**.
		- ### Create personas
			- A **persona** is a **fictional user whose goals and characteristics represent the needs of a larger group of users**.
			- Personas are based on **user research**, not assumptions. Each persona represents a group of users who share similar characteristics, behaviors, goals, and pain points.
			- Personas help UX teams:
				- Keep the **user at the center** of the design process.
				- Understand users' **goals, lifestyles, and challenges**.
				- Identify common **pain points**.
				- Make design decisions based on research.
				- Create designs that speak directly to a specific group of users.
				- Give the entire team a shared understanding of who they are designing for.
			- **How to Build Personas**
				- The basic process is: **User research → Identify themes → Group similar users → Create personas → Use personas to guide design**
				- **1. Conduct user research**
					- Gather information through interviews and other research methods.
				- **2. Identify common themes**
					- Look for recurring goals, behaviors, characteristics, and **pain points**.
				- **3. Group users**
					- Group users who share similar characteristics and themes.
				- **4. Create personas**
					- Create a fictional representation of each significant user group.
				- **5. Use personas in design**
					- Refer back to personas when making design decisions.
			- How many personas?
				- The course suggests that **3–8 personas** are generally enough to represent the majority of a product's user base.
			- **Example**: Daniela
				- **Daniela** represents a group of dog owners using a dog-walking app.
					- ![image.png](../assets/image_1790742102976_0.png)
				- **Design implication**
					- Daniela's concerns can directly influence the design. For example, the app could communicate that **dog walkers are screened and must pass background checks**.
				- The persona therefore connects:
					- **Research → User needs → Design decisions**
			- **Important Principles**
				- 1. Personas must be research-based
					- Don't create a persona simply by imagining your "typical user."
					- Instead: **Research first, then create personas from recurring patterns in the data.**
				- 2. Personas are context-specific
					- A persona should focus on the **behaviors and goals relevant to interacting with the product**.
					- You don't need to document every detail about a user's life.
				- 3. Personas aren't individual users
					- Daniela is fictional. She represents a **larger group of real users** who share similar characteristics, goals, and pain points.
				- 4. Validate team assumptions
					- Before creating personas, ask the team what they think the product's users are like.
					- After creating the personas, compare those assumptions with the research:
						- **Team assumptions ↔ Research data → Validate or contradict assumptions**
					- This helps the team recognize where their assumptions were incorrect.
			- **Key takeaway**
				- A persona is a research-based fictional representation of a group of users with shared goals, behaviors, characteristics, and pain points
	- ## Module 2: Creating user stories and user journey maps
		- ### Craft user stories
			- A **user story** is a **fictional one-sentence story told from a persona's point of view**. It captures what the user wants to do and why, helping UX designers make better design decisions.
			- The standard format is:
				- **As a [type of user], I want to [action], so that [benefit].**
				  | Part | Meaning |
				  | ---- | ---- | ---- |
				  | **Who** | The type of user/persona |
				  | **What** | The action the user wants to take |
				  | **Why** | The benefit or reason for taking the action |
			- Example
				- **As a frequent traveler, I want to hire a consistent dog walker so that I can feel comfortable about the safety of my pets while I'm on vacation.**
					- **Who:** frequent traveler
					- **What:** hire a consistent dog walker
					- **Why:** feel comfortable about pet safety
			- Why are user stories useful?
				- User stories:
					- Put the **user's perspective** at the center of design.
					- Translate persona needs into specific **actions and benefits**.
					- Help designers understand what users need from the product.
					- Act as a **checklist** for whether key user problems are being addressed.
					- Help inform specific design features and functionality.
				- For example, if user stories mention wanting to:
					- request a specific date and time,
					- select a particular dog walker,
					- review background checks,
				- then those needs can inform the app's features.
			- Best Practices
				- Good user stories should be: **Short****, Impactful****, To the point**, Clear about the **action**, Clear about the **benefit**, Written from the **persona/user's point of view**
			- **Personas → User Stories → Design**
				- The course connects these concepts:
				  **User research → Empathy maps → Personas → User stories → Design decisions**
					- **Empathy maps** help understand what users **say, think, do, and feel**.
					- **Personas** organize research into representations of important user groups.
					- **User stories** turn those personas' needs into specific **who + what + why** statements.
					- **Designers use the stories** to decide what the product should enable users to do.
			- Key takeaway
				- **A user story turns a persona's need into a simple statement of who the user is, what they want to do, and why they want to do it.**

		- ### Consider Accessbility
			- **Accessibility** is the design of products, devices, services, or environments for people with disabilities.
			- In UX research, accessibility means considering users with **permanent, temporary, and situational disabilities**.
			- **Don't assume how people with disabilities experience your product. Research with them directly.**
			- **Accessibility Considerations by Ability**
			  | Area | Potential limitation | Design considerations |
			  | ---- | ---- | ---- |
			  | **Touch** | One arm/limited motor ability | Accessible button placement, one-handed keyboard, customizable buttons, avoid accidental clicks |
			  | **Sight** | Limited vision | Larger text, high contrast, alt text, screen-reader support, don't rely on color alone |
			  | **Hear** | Limited hearing | Haptics/vibration, notification lights, captions, text-based communication |
			  | **Speak** | Unable to speak | Written instructions, real-time text, alternatives to voice recognition, in-app messaging |
			  | **Neurodiversity** | Dyslexia, ADHD, etc. | Simple navigation, consistency, familiar icons, combine text with visual/audio explanations |
		- **Terms**:
			- **Curb cut**: The slope of the sidewalk that creates a ramp with adjoining street
			- **Curb cut effect**: A phenomenon that describes how products and policies designed for people with disabilities often end up helping everyone
			- **Customizable text:** A feature that allows users to change how text is displayed in order to read the text more easily
			- **Edge case:** What happens when things go wrong that are beyond the user’s control about a type of user
			- **Happy path**: A user story with a pleasant ending
			- **User journey:** The series of experiences a user has as they achieve a specific goal
			- **User story:** A fictional one-sentence story told from the persona’s point of view to inspire and inform design decisions
	- ## Module 3: Defining user problems
		- ### Define problem and hypothesis statements
			- The **Define phase** builds on the research and empathy work from the **Empathize phase**.
			- Designers use what they learned about users to clearly define **the most important problems** and begin thinking about possible solutions.
			- To answer this question, you’ll use **problem statements, hypothesis statements**, and, finally, **goal statements**.
			- Previous activities provide the foundation:
				- **Interviews → Empathy maps → Personas → User stories + Journey maps → Problem definition → Solutions**
			- User stories and journey maps act as a **bridge between the Empathize and Define phases**.
			- In the Define phase, designers answer two key questions:
				- **Which user needs or problems are most important to address?**
				- **How might the design address those needs effectively and uniquely?**
			- **1. Problem Statements**
				- A **problem statement** summarizes:
					- **Who** the user is
					- **What** they need
					- **Why** they need it
				- **Formula**
					- **[Persona] is a [type of user] who needs [type of user experience] because [benefit].**
			- Example:
				- Berta **is an** older person with a visual impairment **who needs** a website and online ordering system that is easy to use, adapts to her vision needs, and feels like a friendly phone conversation.
				- The problem statement focuses on **the user's problem and needs**, rather than immediately proposing a specific solution.
			- **2. Hypothesis Statements**
				- A **hypothesis statement** moves from defining the problem toward **ideating a possible solution**.
				- It is an educated guess about what solution might address the identified problem.
				- Formula
					- **If [user] can perform [action], then they will achieve [benefit].**
				- Example
					- **If** Berta **can** place online orders through a simple system that mimics a friendly phone conversation, **she will** continue to feel connected to the bakery.
					  Think of it as:
					- **Problem → Proposed action → Expected benefit**
			- **3. Value Propositions**
				- A **value proposition** is a short, compelling statement explaining **how a product addresses user needs and creates unique value**.
				- It answers two questions:
					- **What does the product do?** Explain the key actions or benefits the product provides.
					- **Why should the user care?** Explain how the product addresses their pain points in a **unique or valuable way**.
				- Example
					- The bakery's website provides a streamlined online ordering experience that feels like a friendly phone conversation, allowing customers to order online while maintaining a sense of personal connection with bakery staff.
			- **How They Connect**
				- These three concepts build on each other:
				  | Concept | Main question | Purpose |
				  | ---- | ---- | ---- |
				  | **Problem statement** | What problem does the user have? | Define the user's need |
				  | **Hypothesis statement** | What might solve the problem? | Propose/test a possible solution |
				  | **Value proposition** | Why is this solution valuable? | Explain the product's unique user benefit |
				- **Simple flow**
					- **Research → Understand users → Identify problem → Hypothesize solution → Define value → Ideate**
			- **Key takeaway**
				- **The Define phase transforms empathy and research into clearly defined user problems and potential solutions.**
				- Remember the three important outputs:
					- **Problem statement = WHO + NEED + WHY**
					- **Hypothesis = IF ACTION → THEN BENEFIT**
					- **Value proposition = WHAT THE PRODUCT DOES + WHY THE USER SHOULD CARE**
			- **Define Problem Statements**
				- A **problem statement** is a clear description of the **user's need that should be addressed**.
				- Identifying the user's **pain points** is the starting point. Pain points are UX issues or friction that **frustrate users and prevent them from getting what they need**.
				- The goal is not simply to identify *what is wrong*, but to understand the problem **from the user's point of view**.
				- **Pain Points → Problem Statement**
				- There are four categories of pain points:
				  | Category | Meaning |
				  | ---- | ---- | ---- |
				  | **Financial** | Money-related problems |
				  | **Product** | Product quality or design problems |
				  | **Process** | Problems with the user's journey or process |
				  | **Support** | Problems getting help or customer service |
				- Once pain points are identified, designers can use them to determine the underlying user problem that the design should address.
				- **Example: Arnold**
					- Arnold is a busy executive who:
						- Works more than 60 hours per week.
						- Cares for three dogs.
						- Needs a daily dog walker.
						- Is not technologically savvy.
						- Struggles to navigate the dog-walker app.
					- His main pain point is a **process pain point** because he has difficulty completing the app journey.
				- **The 5 Ws and H Framework**
					- The course introduces **5 Ws + H** as a framework for understanding the full context of a user's problem.
					  | Question | What to determine |
					  | ---- | ---- | ---- |
					  | **Who?** | Who is experiencing the problem? |
					  | **What?** | What pain point are you trying to solve? |
					  | **Where?** | Where is the user when using the product? |
					  | **When?** | When does the problem occur? |
					  | **Why?** | Why is the problem important to the user? |
					  | **How?** | How does the user currently reach their goal? |
				- **Arnold's example**
					- **Who:** A busy executive
					- **What:** Wants to hire a daily dog walker for his three dogs
					- **Where:** Likely using the app at work or while on the go
					- **When:** Gets frustrated when opening the app and beginning the user journey
					- **Why:** Has little experience with phone apps and similar technology
					- **How:** Wants to easily move from the home screen → dog walker list → confirmation
					- This framework forces designers to consider the **entire context of the problem**, rather than focusing only on the obvious frustration.
				- Problem Statement Formula
					- **[User] is a [type of user] who needs [need] because [reason/insight].**
					- For Arnold:
						- **Arnold is a busy executive who needs an easy app experience to hire a dog walker because he's not technologically savvy.**
						- Notice that the statement doesn't immediately prescribe a detailed solution. It identifies:
						- **Who → Need → Why**
				- Why Problem Statements Matter
					- Problem statements help UX designers:
						- Clarify **user goals**.
						- Identify **constraints** preventing users from achieving those goals.
						- Keep the design focused on the **actual user problem**.
						- Help the team determine what a successful solution should accomplish.
						- Provide a basis for measuring whether the design is effective.
						- For Arnold, the design should therefore prioritize an experience that is **clear and easy to understand**.
				- **Important distinction**
				  "The user is struggling."
				  to:
				  "Let's add feature X."
				  Instead:
				  **Research → Pain point → 5 Ws + H → Problem statement → Design solution**
				  The **problem statement defines the problem before the designer decides how to solve it.**
				- **Key takeaway**
					- A strong problem statement captures the user's perspective, clearly defines their need, and explains why that need matters.
					- Remember:
						- **Pain point = What is frustrating the user**
						  **5 Ws + H = Understand the full context**
						  **Problem statement = Clearly define the user's need to solve**
			- **Define Hypothesis Statements**
				- A hypothesis statement is an educated guess about a potential solution to a design problem. It builds on user research and problem statements to identify ways a design could address users' pain points.
				- The process so far:
				- Empathize → Define the problem → Hypothesize solutions
					- Empathize: Understand users' experiences, preferences, and pain points through research.
					- Define: Organize research insights into a clear problem statement.
					- Hypothesize: Propose potential solutions that address the problem.
				- **Two Common Formats**
					- 1. If / Then format
						- Focuses directly on the user's needs and what might happen if a solution is available.
						  Formula:
						  **If [user/action], then [expected outcome].**
						- Examples from the dog-walker app:
							- If Arnold downloads the app, then they can use a simplified mode to view only basic app functions.
							- If Tobias signs up for the app, then they can quickly choose a dog walker who fits their schedule.
					- 2. We Believe format
					- Expresses the design team's belief about how a proposed solution will benefit a particular user.
					- Formula:
					  **We believe that [solution] for [user] will [expected benefit].**
					  Examples:
						- We believe that a simplified app mode for Arnold will allow them to hire dog walkers efficiently.
						- We believe that easy access to available dog walkers for Tobias will increase the number of walks they choose for their pets.
					- **Key Differences**
					  | If / Then | We Believe |
					  | Focuses directly on the user and the expected outcome | Expresses the team's belief about a solution |
					  | Emphasizes what may happen if the user can do something | Emphasizes how a proposed design may benefit the user |
				- **Key takeaway**
					- A hypothesis statement connects a user problem to a potential solution and its expected benefit.
					- Remember:
						- Problem statement: What does the user need, and why?
						- Hypothesis statement: What solution might address that need, and what benefit do we expect?
						- Hypotheses are educated guesses, not proven facts. They help guide design decisions and keep the team focused on users' needs.
			- **Review:  Build  Problem  Statements  and  Hypothesis  Statements**
				- **1.  Problem  Statements**
					- A strong problem statement should be:
						- Human-centered: Focused on a specific user's needs.
						- Broad enough: Allows designers creative freedom to explore different solutions.
						- Narrow enough: Can be addressed by a practical design solution.
					- Template
					  **[User name] is a/an [user characteristics] who needs [user need] because [actionable insight].**
					  Steps to create one:
						- Identify the persona.
						- Describe their characteristics.
						- Clearly state their need.
						- Explain why they have that need, using an insight that the design can address.
					- Example:  CoffeeHouse  app
					  Problem statement:
					  Anika is a busy marketing intern who needs a faster, more collaborative way to collect multiple coffee orders from coworkers because taking individual orders takes too much time.
					  This statement helps the team define:
						- Goal: Enable efficient collaborative group ordering.
						- Constraints: Users need to place orders quickly.
						- Deliverables: A prototype for a collaborative ordering system.
						- Success benchmarks: Faster ordering and less time spent completing group orders.
				- **2.  Hypothesis  Statements**
					- A hypothesis statement proposes a potential solution and predicts its expected outcome.
					- Template
					  **If [action], then [outcome].**
					  Steps to create one:
						- Determine an action that could address the user's need.
						- Specify the outcome you expect from that action.
						  The if/then format is common, but it is not mandatory. Reword the statement if another format communicates the idea more naturally.
					- Example:  Anika
					  Hypothesis statement:
					  If Anika uses the CoffeeHouse app to collect and submit orders, then they can get coffee for everyone in the office quickly and easily.
					- Goal: Enable Anika to collect and submit group orders.
					- Success criteria: Speed, efficiency, and completeness of the orders.
				- **3.  How  Problem  and  Hypothesis  Statements  Connect**
					- The hypothesis should directly address the problem identified in the problem statement.
					- **Problem  statement  —  Ali**
					  Ali is a remote student who needs to place and receive orders without leaving their table because someone else might take their seat.
					- **Hypothesis  statement  —  Ali**
					  If Ali uses the CoffeeHouse app to place an in-store order from their table, then an employee can deliver the order so Ali won't lose their seat.
					  
					  The solution directly addresses the user's specific problem: ordering without losing their table.
				- **4.  Review  Checklist**
					- Before finalizing your statements, ask:
					- Problem statement
						- Is it human-centered and focused on a specific persona?
						- Is it broad enough to allow creative freedom?
						- Is it narrow enough to solve with a practical design?
					- Hypothesis statement
						- Does it identify a specific action the solution enables?
						- Does it state the desired outcome?
						- Does that outcome help determine whether the user's need has been met?
				- **Key  Takeaways**
					- | Concept | Purpose |
					  | Problem statement | Defines the user's need and actionable insight |
					  | Hypothesis statement | Proposes an action and predicts its outcome |
					  | Success criteria | Help evaluate whether the solution meets the user's need |
					  Remember: Start with a well-defined user problem, then create a hypothesis that directly addresses it. These statements align the design team around a clear goal and help keep design work focused, practical, and user-centered.
		- ### Create a value proposition
			- **A value proposition** explains why users should use a product or service, including how it meets their needs and what makes it valuable or different.
			- Example: Gmail
				- Free email
				- Spam filtering
				- Email organization
				- Unique value at launch: email conversation views and 1 GB of storage, which distinguished Gmail from competitors at the time.
			- To start, you need to do some research in order to answer these two questions:
				- **What does your product do?** Clearly explain the offering that your product provides users.
				- **Why should the user care?** Describe how your product addresses users’ pain points.
			- **How  to  Build  a  Value  Proposition**
				- **List features and benefits.** Brainstorm everything the product offers.
				  ![vp1.png](../assets/vp1_1791692029789_0.png)
				- **Group them by user value.** Use research and interviews to identify what matters to users. For the dog-walker app: accessibility, professionalism, cost, and reliability.
				  ![vp2.png](../assets/vp2_1791692063811_0.png)
				- **Match values to user needs.** Connect features to personas and their biggest pain points.
				  ![vp3.png](../assets/vp3_1791692088027_0.png)
				- **Review and prioritize.** Keep benefits that address real user needs.
				- **Identify the unique value proposition (UVP).** Compare competitors and focus on unmet needs, using competitor reviews to discover weaknesses.
				  ![vp4.png](../assets/vp4_1791692097116_0.png)
				- Example:
					- Users complain that competing dog-walking apps lack proper walker training and reliability. An app that provides thorough, in-person training and dependable walkers could stand out.
			- **Key  Takeaways**
				- A feature is not automatically valuable just because it exists.
				- Value propositions should be based on user research, personas, and pain points.
				- A unique value proposition (UVP) highlights a benefit that differentiates the product from competitors.
				- Keep value propositions short, clear, and specific so users quickly understand why the product matters.
			- **Remember:** List features → identify user value → match user needs → analyze competitors → define your unique value.
		- ### Explore the influence of psychology in UX design
			- **Human Factors in Design**
				- Human factors refer to the range of human variables that affect how people interact with products, such as impatience, limited memory, and making errors.
				- Common human factors that inform design:
					- Impatience
					- Limited memory
					- Need for analogies
					- Limited concentration
					- Changes in needs
					- Need for motivation
					- Prejudices and fears
					- Making errors
					- Misjudgment
			- **Mental models**
				- **Mental models** are internal maps that help users predict how things work, like expecting a door to open and allow passage.
				- **Feedback loops** provide users with confirmation of their actions, such as a light turning on when a switch is flipped, reinforcing expectations.
				- Design Opportunities from Human Limitations
					- Designers can leverage human tendencies, like nostalgia, to create emotional connections with users, such as reissuing classic packaging designs.
					- Understanding human limitations allows UX designers to create better, more user-friendly experiences by turning constraints into opportunities.
			- **Psychological Phenomena Impacting UX**
				- **Color Psychology**: Colors influence emotions and behavior.
				- **Von Restorff Effect**: Unique elements stand out and are more memorable. Which is why call-to-action buttons are designed to stand out visually.
				- **Serial Position Effect**: People remember the first and last items best.
				- **Hick’s Law**: More choices increase decision time.
	- ## Module 4: Ideating design solutions
		- ### Understand design ideation
			- **Design ideation** is the third phase of design thinking. It involves generating many possible solutions to a clearly defined user problem without judging or evaluating ideas too early.
			- The goal is to explore different possibilities, including unusual ideas, rather than settling on the first obvious solution.
			- **Prepare  for  Ideation**
				- Before ideating, complete the first two phases:
					- **Empathize**: Understand users through empathy maps, personas, user stories, and journey maps.
					- **Define**: Clearly identify the user problem so the team focuses on the same need.
			- **Tips  for  Effective  Ideation**
				- Create a creative environment: Choose a comfortable space with minimal distractions.
				- Set a time limit: Allow enough time to brainstorm, but set a stopping point to evaluate ideas.
				- Build a diverse team: Different backgrounds, abilities, and perspectives encourage varied ideas.
				- Think outside the box: Welcome unconventional ideas and avoid limiting yourself to familiar solutions.
			- **Key ideation techniques:**
				- https://careerfoundry.com/en/blog/ux-design/what-is-ideation-in-design-thinking/
			- **Key takeaway:**
				- Empathize with users → Define the problem → Generate many ideas → Evaluate and narrow them down. The goal is to explore broadly before deciding which solutions to pursue.
		- ### Use research to inform ideation
			- https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-ideation/
		- ### Build goal statements
			- A **goal statement** is one or two sentences describing a product, what it helps users do, and how it benefits them. It turns the user’s problem into a focused design goal.
			- It defines:
				- Who: Who the product serves.
				- What: What users can do with the product.
				- Why: How the product meets their needs.
				- Measurement: How success will be evaluated.
			- **How  to  Create  a  Goal  Statement**
				- **Step 1: Revisit the problem statement.**
					- Template: [User] **is a** [user characteristics] **who needs** [user need] **because** [insight].
					- Example: Sawyer **is a** construction business owner **who needs** to attend local trade conferences **because** they need to network to find more clients.
					- You can also gather information from existing research:
						- Persona → Who
						- User story → What
						- Empathy map and journey map → Why
				- **Step 2: Define the desired solution.**
					- Use this template:
						- Our [product] **will let users** [specific action], **which will affect** [who is affected] **by** [positive impact]. **We will measure effectiveness by** [success metric].
					- Example:
						- Our TradeConference app **will let users** expand their business **which will affect** how business owners connect with new clients **by** giving them the ability to connect with clients at local trade shows. **We will measure effectiveness by** analyzing show attendance.
			- **What  Makes  a  Strong  Goal  Statement?**
				- A strong goal statement should:
					- Describe a specific action users can take.
					- Identify who benefits from the action.
					- Explain the positive impact on users.
					- Define measurable success criteria.
			- **Key takeaway:**
				- A problem statement explains the user's need; a goal statement describes the intended solution and how success will be measured. Use both to keep design work focused on user needs.
		- ### Conduct competitive audits
			- A **competitive audit** is an overview of competitors’ strengths and weaknesses. It helps UX designers understand the market and design products that better meet users’ needs and stand out.
			- **Types  of  competitors**
				- Direct competitors: Offer similar products to the same target audience.
				- Indirect competitors: Offer similar products to a different audience, or different products to the same audience.
			- **Why  Are  Competitive  Audits  Important?**
			- Competitive audits help you:
				- Understand existing products and their designs.
				- Find ideas for solving design problems.
				- Identify gaps where competitors fail to meet users’ needs.
				- Understand product life cycles and how competitors’ products evolve.
				- Learn from competitors’ successful and unsuccessful design iterations.
			- **How  to  Prepare**
				- Identify your direct and indirect competitors.
				- Decide which **aspects** of your design to investigate, such as navigation, features, or user flows.
				- Examine competitors’ products and user feedback.
				- Use your findings to identify opportunities for improvement and differentiation.
			- UX designers may collaborate with marketing and product management teams during this process.
			- **Key takeaway:** Learn from competitors’ strengths and weaknesses, avoid repeating their mistakes, and identify unmet user needs that your product can address uniquely.
			- **The  5  Steps  of  a  Competitive  Audit**
				- The lesson uses Pizza Garden, a fast-food pizza restaurant redesign project, as an example.
					- https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/161VBzpqjfF1X9hjp9E5TlbLXOuFrBS4KQsrQke8SZlk/template/preview
					- [competitor_audit.xlsx](../assets/competitor_audit_1791826771955_0.xlsx)
				- **1. Outline the audit goals**
				  id:: 6acd1b54-5b74-4abf-838e-07ab0a77c7db
					- Define what you want to learn before starting.
					- For a website redesign, the goal might be to compare competitors’ website user experiences.
					- Use consistent rating criteria:
						- **Needs work**: The experience is unusable or fails to meet user needs.
						- **Okay**: Some issues need improvement.
						- **Good**: Works well, but some information or tasks could be easier.
						- **Outstanding**: Consistently meets or exceeds user needs.
				- **2. List competitors**
					- Identify both types of competitors:
						- Direct competitors: Similar products or services targeting the same audience.
						- Indirect competitors: Similar offerings for a different audience, or different offerings for the same audience.
					- For Pizza Garden, direct competitors include Pie Eaters, Pizza Nest, and The Spotty Pie. The Vegan Oven is an indirect competitor but still offers useful design insights.
				- **3. Determine what to compare**
					- Choose consistent categories for evaluating every competitor:
						- **First impressions**: Visual appeal, initial reactions, and responsiveness across desktop and mobile.
						- **Interaction**: Navigation, user flows, accessibility, and ease of finding information.
						- **Visual design**: Branding, colors, typography, imagery, and consistency.
						- **Content**: Tone, clarity, length, and how easily users can find relevant information.
				- **4. Research each company**
					- Examine each competitor using the same categories. Record observations using notes, links, and screenshots.
					- The Pizza Garden example reveals several opportunities:
						- First impressions: Some websites have appealing imagery and responsive layouts, while others have poor layouts or mobile display issues.
						- Interaction: Animations and unnecessary homepage content can make navigation harder. Clear navigation, obvious clickable elements, screen-reader compatibility, and multiple language options can improve usability.
						- Visual design: Consistent colors, typography, and photography help communicate a brand. Inconsistent styling weakens it.
						- Content: Short, clear, engaging descriptions are generally easier to use than long, dry, or inconsistent copy.
				- **5. Summarize findings in a report**
					- Organize the research into a report that supports design decisions. The format depends on the audit goals:
						- Use charts and figures when comparing audiences or numerical information.
						- Use screenshots and annotations when comparing visual design.
						- Highlight strengths, weaknesses, recurring patterns, and opportunities for improvement.
					- Template Example:
						- https://docs.google.com/document/d/1lExH--Wky12432L7lUCkd6M7JOCLU7QTrlUgrOafnCg/template/preview#heading=h.ro6simovwtk5
						- The report should answer these eight questions:
							- **1. What are your audit goals?**
							  State the goals from your audit spreadsheet.
							- **2. Who are your key competitors?**
							  Describe each competitor briefly and label it direct or indirect.
							- **3. What are the type and quality of their products?**
							  Summarize what they offer and what works well or needs improvement, using about 2–5 sentences per competitor.
							- **4. How do competitors position themselves?**
							  Describe their target audiences and ideal customers.
							- **5. How do competitors talk about themselves?**
							  Summarize each competitor’s unique value proposition.
							- **6. What are their strengths?**
							  List 2–4 things each competitor does particularly well.
							- **7. What are their weaknesses?**
							  List 2–4 areas where each competitor could improve.

			- 2 type:
				- **Pragmatic way: How Might We**
				- **Creative way: Crazy Eights / Rapid Sketching**
			- **How Might We (HMW)** is a design thinking technique used during the **ideation stage** to reframe user problems as opportunities for design. It encourages designers to explore different perspectives and generate a wide variety of possible solutions.
			- **How  to  Frame  HMW  Questions**
				- Start with a clearly defined problem statement, then turn it into questions beginning with “How might we…?”
				- Using Darren’s problem as an example: he needs to keep track of his concert ticket because he needs it to pass through security.
			- The lesson introduces eight ways to reframe the problem:
				- | Angles | Technique | Example HMW question |
				  |Amp up the good| Turn a negative into a positive | How might we make keeping track of tickets fun and competitive? |
				  | Explore the opposite | Consider the opposite | How might we create a way to lose tickets? |
				  | Change a status quo | Change the process | How might we make a non-paper concert ticket? |
				  | Break the point-of-view into pieces | Break a complex problem into smaller parts | How might we keep a ticket from getting lost? How might we make a lost ticket easier for security to handle? |
				  | Remove the bad | Remove the negative entirely | How might concertgoers enter a venue without needing a ticket? |
				  | Go after the adjective | Turn negative adjectives into positive ones | How might we make concert entry less stressful? |
				  | Question an assumption | Challenge assumed processes | How might we remove the security check process at a concert? |
				  | Create an analogy using the established need or context | Compare with another experience or use another resource | How might we make security feel like a video game? How might facial recognition help manage concert entry? |
			- **Best  Practices  for  Writing  HMW  Questions**
				- Be broad: Allow multiple possible solutions instead of suggesting only one.
				- Avoid being too broad: Keep the question focused on a specific user problem. For example, “How might we make ticketing better?” is too vague.
				- Create multiple drafts: Rewrite questions that fail to inspire useful ideas.
				- Be creative: Explore imaginative and unconventional possibilities.
				- Write as many HMW questions as possible: More questions can lead to a wider range of potential solutions.
			- **Key  Takeaway**
				- A good HMW question is open-ended enough to inspire multiple solutions but focused enough to guide useful ideas. Reframe the problem from different angles, draft several questions, and explore possibilities before choosing a solution.
			- **Rapid Sketching**, also known as **Crazy Eights**, is a design ideation exercise that helps UX designers generate multiple possible solutions to a user problem in a short amount of time.
			- The goal is to explore many ideas quickly, not to create polished designs.
			  ![crazy-8s-activities.jpg](../assets/crazy-8s-activities_1791862058203_0.jpg)
			- **How  to  Do  Rapid  Sketching**
				- Prepare the paper: Fold a large sheet of paper in half three times to create eight squares.
				- Get drawing materials: Use a pen, pencil, or marker.
				- Set a timer: Allow eight minutes total, or one minute per sketch.
				- Sketch eight ideas: Draw a different potential solution in each square. Don't worry about artistic quality.
				- Review as a group: Each participant shares their top two or three ideas, or the team votes on favorite sketches.
				- Develop the best ideas: Expand the selected concepts into more detailed sketches.
			- For example, five people sketching simultaneously can generate 40 potential design solutions in just eight minutes.
			- **Best  Practices**
				- Warm up creatively: Start with a simple drawing exercise to get comfortable putting ideas on paper.
				- Define the problem clearly: Focus on one problem statement or one How Might We (HMW) question per exercise.
				- Avoid judging your ideas: Generate as many possibilities as you can, even unconventional ones.
				- Respect others' ideas: Stay open-minded when teammates share their sketches.
				- Include diverse participants: Different backgrounds, abilities, experiences, and professional roles encourage varied perspectives.
				- Choose a comfortable environment: Work somewhere relaxed and conducive to creative thinking.
				- Don't worry about artistic ability: Stick figures and simple drawings are enough, as long as they communicate the idea clearly.
			- **Benefits  of  Rapid  Sketching**
				- Generates many ideas quickly: Produces multiple potential solutions in a short time.
				- Encourages creative thinking: Time constraints help you explore ideas beyond the obvious.
				- Promotes diverse solutions: Delaying judgment allows unconventional concepts to emerge.
				- Supports collaboration: Teams can share, compare, vote on, and develop one another's ideas.
			- **Key  Takeaway**
				- Rapid Sketching is about quantity before quality. Define the problem, sketch eight different ideas in eight minutes, and postpone judgment until the ideation process is complete. Then collaborate to select and develop the most promising concepts.

				- A prototype is a visual representation of a product that shows how it looks, what users can interact with, and how it is expected to function.

				- Communicate ideas to stakeholders and developers.
				- Test designs before development.

Prototypes are iterations, not final products—their purpose is to identify problems and improve the user experience before launch.

			- A **user flow** is the path a typical user takes through an app or website to complete a task from start to finish.
			- UX designers create user flows before designing screens to understand:
				- What actions users will take.
				- What decisions users will make.
				- Which screens users will encounter.
			- User flows help designers anticipate user needs and create simple, efficient experiences.
			- **Common User Flow Symbols**
			  | Shape | Meaning | Example |
			  | ---- | ---- | ---- |
			  | Circle | **Action** performed by the user | Open an app or book a dog walker |
			  | Rectangle | **Screen** displayed in the product | Homepage or booking confirmation |
			  | Diamond | **Decision** made by the user | Decide whether to book a dog walker |
			  | Lines with arrows | **Direction of flow** | Connect actions, screens, and decisions |
			- **Solid lines:** Show forward movement through the flow.
			- **Dotted lines:** Show backward movement or returning to a previous page.
			- **How to Create a User Flow**
				- **Understand user needs:** Refer to user research and the problem statement.
				- **Identify the user's goal:** Determine what task the user wants to complete.**
				- **Map actions and screens:** Outline the steps users take and the screens they encounter.
				- **Add decision points:** Account for choices that may lead users down different paths.
				- **Consider alternative paths:** Include important additional needs, such as accessing tips before booking.
				- **Review and refine:** Ensure the flow addresses the needs of the majority of users.
			- **Example: Dog Walker App**
				- A typical user flow might be:
					- Open app → View homepage → Browse dog walker profiles → Select a dog walker → Book an appointment → View booking confirmation.

					- Users might also return to browse other profiles before deciding. If users need hiring tips, the flow could include an alternative path to a tips screen.
			- **Important Considerations**
				- **Design around user needs:** Use research and the problem statement to guide the flow.
				- **Expect changes:** Update the user flow when research reveals new user needs or the problem statement changes.
				- **Focus on common scenarios:** You don't need to map every possible situation, but you should cover the needs most users are likely to encounter.
				- **Improve existing products too:** Research can help reorganize, add, or remove screens to make an established user flow more effective.

				- A user flow maps how users move through a product to achieve their goals. By outlining actions, screens, decisions, and alternative paths **before designing the interface**, UX designers can create more intuitive and user-centered experiences.

		- ### Create storyboards
			- **What Is a Storyboard?**
			  A **storyboard** is a series of visual panels that describe a user's experience with a product. It focuses on the most important moments of the user's journey and shows how a design solves their problem.    Pasted text(3)
			- A storyboard has four key elements:
				- **Character:** The user in the story.
				- **Scene:** The user's environment or context.
				- **Plot:** The solution or benefit provided by the design.
				- **Narrative:** The user's problem and how the design solves it.
			- **Two Types of Storyboards**
			  | Type | Focus | Best used |
			  | ---- | ---- | ---- |
			  | **Big-picture storyboard** | The user's needs, context, feelings, and why the product is useful | Early in the design process |
			  | **Close-up storyboard** | Product details, interactions, screens, and how the product works | Middle to late in the design process |
			- **How to Create a Storyboard**
				- 1–4: Prepare the Storyboard
					- **Write a problem statement** to identify the user and their needs.
						- Example: Dan **is a** lead guitarist in a band **who needs** to hire a new drummer **because** they are having issues replacing the previous drummer.
					- **Create a goal statement** to define the solution and its benefits.
						- Example: Our Bandmate app **will let users** recruit new or substitute musicians **which will affect** users who need new band members **by** letting them easily find qualified musicians to hire. **We will measure effectiveness by** reading user reviews and tracking successful hires.
					- **Set up a storyboard template** with spaces for illustrations and descriptions.
						- https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/135qtZ8ERI7Ldbt8q9rt_ZHsCwKnbyNJRyX1AY2YxV5E/template/preview
					- **Write the scenario** that explains the user's problem and the product's purpose.
				- Step 5: Draw One Idea per Panel
					- Each panel should represent one important moment in the user's journey.
					- For a **big-picture storyboard**, focus on the user's experience, actions, and emotions.
					- For a **close-up storyboard**, focus on product interactions, screens, and how each action leads to the next step.
				- Steps 6–7: Complete the User's Journey
					- For a big-picture storyboard:
						- Highlight **user pain points and challenges**.
						- Show **how the product addresses those problems**.
						- End with the **user's goal achieved** and show **how they feel**.
			- **Example: Bandmate App**
				- The Bandmate app helps musicians find qualified band members.
				- **Big-picture storyboard:**
					- Dan's drummer leaves the band.
					- Dan opens the Bandmate app.
					- He searches for experienced drummers nearby.
					- He checks candidates' qualifications and reviews.
					- He selects a candidate and schedules an interview.
					- A new drummer joins the band, and Dan feels satisfied.
					- ![storyboard.png](../assets/storyboard_1793001900277_0.png)
				- **Close-up storyboard:**
					- The user opens the app.
					- They create a profile or log in.
					- They search and filter musician profiles.
					- They view a musician's qualifications.
					- They send a message to a potential bandmate.
					- They receive confirmation that the message was sent.
					- ![storyboard2.png](../assets/storyboard2_1793001907368_0.png)
				- The big-picture storyboard emphasizes **why the product helps the user**, while the close-up storyboard demonstrates **how the product works**.
			- **Key Takeaway**
				- Storyboards help UX designers visualize the user journey before building a product. Start with a problem statement and goal statement, then create panels showing the user's experience.
				- **Remember:** Big-picture storyboards **focus on the user and their needs**; close-up storyboards **focus on the product and its interactions**.
		- ### Get started with wireframes
			- **What Are Wireframes?**
				- **Wireframes** are simplified representations of a product's structure that show the placement and intended function of its elements.
				- They help designers ensure that:
					- All necessary elements are included.
					- No elements are missing or misplaced.
					- The layout is organized and understandable.
				- Creating paper wireframes from existing apps is a useful way to practice before designing your own products.
			- **How to Draw a Paper Wireframe**
				- **Step 1: Choose an App**
				  Select a familiar app with common UI components, such as menus, buttons, and images. The lesson uses the YouTube home screen as an example.
				- **Step 2: Draw the Screen Frame**
				  Draw a large rectangle representing the app screen. Its dimensions do not need to be exact, but the proportions should approximate the original screen.
				- **Step 3: Sketch the Key Elements**
				  Use simple shapes and follow these conventions:
				  | UI Element | Wireframe Representation |
				  | ---- | ---- | ---- |
				  | Body text | Horizontal lines |
				  | Short labels and headings | Written text |
				  | Images, photos, and illustrations | Squares or rectangles with a large X |
				  | Icons | Simple shapes resembling the original icons |
				  | Calls to action (buttons) | Rectangles or circles |
				- ![yt_wf.jpeg](../assets/yt_wf_1793073542051_0.jpeg)
				  Following these conventions makes wireframes easier for designers and collaborators to understand.
			- **Assess Your Wireframe Holistically**
				- After drawing, review the entire wireframe to check whether:
				- All key elements fit inside the screen frame.
				- The wireframe represents the original screen's important elements.
				- Text, images, icons, and buttons follow standard wireframe conventions.
				- Elements are proportionally sized and well organized.
				- Reviewing the whole layout is important, even if you have checked individual elements while drawing.
			- **Key Takeaway**
				- Drawing wireframes from existing apps helps you practice identifying screen structures and representing UI elements using simple shapes. **Keep wireframes simple, organized, proportional, and consistent with industry conventions.** These skills are useful for portfolio projects and collaboration with other UX designers.

		- ### Understand information architecture
			- **What Is Information Architecture (IA)?**
				- **Information architecture (IA)** is the process of organizing information so users can easily navigate and interact with an app or website. Think of it as a map of your product.
			- IA has three main components:
				- **Organization:** How information is grouped and connected.
				- **Hierarchy:** How information is arranged from broad categories to specific subcategories using parent-child relationships.
				- **Sequence:** The order or steps users follow to navigate through a product.
				- These components help UX designers create a **sitemap**, a visual representation of the product's structure.
			- **The Eight Principles of Information Architecture**
				- https://medium.com/@mattholla/the-eight-principles-of-information-architecture-6feff11f907a
			- **Creating a Sitemap**
				- A **sitemap** organizes an app or website into a hierarchy of screens and their relationships.
				- In this structure, the parent represents a higher-level category, while its children represent related pages or options. Items at the same level are peers.
			- **Mobile App Sitemaps**
				- Mobile apps use a similar structure but generally have fewer high-level categories.
				- ![sitemap_mobile.png](../assets/sitemap_mobile_1793154472382_0.png)
			- **Information Architecture for Mobile Apps**
				- Because mobile screens are smaller, UX designers should:
					- Prioritize essential content and remove or relocate less important information.
					- Ensure touchscreen elements have appropriate sizes and spacing.
					- Make text large enough to read comfortably.
					- The structure should reflect the app's purpose and users' needs, making important information easy to find.
			- **Key Takeaway**
				- Information architecture helps UX designers organize content into a logical structure that supports intuitive navigation. By applying the eight principles and creating a clear sitemap, designers can build products that are **easy to navigate, accessible, and flexible enough to grow**.

			- **What Are Paper Wireframes?**
			  A **wireframe** is a low-fidelity outline of a digital product, such as a website or mobile app. It focuses on the basic page structure and the intended function of each element rather than detailed visual design.
			- **Benefits of wireframing:**
				- Identify the content and elements a product needs.
				- Detect design problems early.
				- Help stakeholders focus on structure and functionality.
				- Save time and resources.
				- Make it easier to explore and improve ideas quickly.

				- The CoffeeHouse app example demonstrates the following process:
				- **Step 1: Review Existing Research and Design Artifacts**
				- Before drawing, review:
					- User research and the problem statement.
					- Storyboards that illustrate the user's experience.
					- User flows that map the steps users take to achieve their goals.
					- Ideas generated during rapid sketching.
					- These materials help ensure that wireframes address actual user needs.
				- **Step 2: Gather Materials**
					- Plain paper, preferably 8.5 × 11 inches or larger.
					- A pencil or another writing utensil.
				- **Step 3: Create a List of Required Elements**
					- List the elements that should appear on the screen before sketching. This helps prevent important features from being overlooked.
					- For the CoffeeHouse app homepage, the list includes:
						- Navigation
						- Search feature
						- Shopping cart or bag
						- Images
						- Text
				- **Step 4: Sketch at Least Five Versions**
					- Create **at least five different versions of the same screen**, exploring different ways to organize its information.

					- For each version:
						- Draw a rectangle representing the phone screen.
						- Add the elements from your list.
						- Experiment with different layouts and arrangements.

		- ### Use Gestalt Principles in wireframes
			- **What  Are  Gestalt  Principles?**
				- Gestalt Principles describe how the human brain groups similar elements, recognizes patterns, and simplifies complex visual information.
				- Rather than treating design as purely subjective, designers should consider the user's goals, the problems they need to solve, and how the design guides their actions.
			- **Three  Gestalt  Principles  for  Wireframing**
				- **1. Similarity**
					- Elements that look alike in shape, size, or color are perceived as having the same function.
					- Example: Use avatar placeholders with the same shape and size when each avatar links to a user profile.
					  ![similarity.svg](../assets/similarity_1793171273250_0.svg)
					- UX benefit: Creates consistency and helps users recognize similar functions quickly.
				- **2. Proximity**
					- Elements positioned close together are perceived as more related than elements placed farther apart.
					- Example: Place a “Learn More” button near the article it relates to.
					  ![proximity.svg](../assets/proximity_1793171278586_0.svg)
					- UX benefit: Makes relationships between content and actions clearer, reducing confusion and hesitation.
				- **3. Common Region**
					- Elements inside the same enclosed area are perceived as belonging together.
					- Example: Place navigation icons inside a shared bar with a background color or border.
					  ![common_region.svg](../assets/common_region_1793171302096_0.svg)
					- UX benefit: Visually groups important navigation items and distinguishes them from other screen elements.
			- **How  to  Apply  These  Principles**
				- When creating wireframes, ask yourself:
					- Similarity: Do elements with the same function look consistent?
					- Proximity: Are related content and actions positioned close together?
					- Common region: Are related elements grouped inside a clear visual boundary?
				- These principles can be applied even in low-fidelity wireframes. You do not need polished colors or detailed graphics to communicate structure and relationships.

				  | **Interactivity** | Typically static. | Interactive; users can click elements to navigate between screens. |
				  | **Connections** | Screens may be designed separately. | Screens are connected to represent a user journey. |
				  | **Testing** | Review whether the layout contains the necessary elements and supports user needs. | Test whether interactions, navigation, and task completion work as intended. |
				  | **Output** | A set of screen layouts. | A connected, interactive set of screens. |
				- **Example: Coffee Ordering App**
					- **Wireframe:**
						- Shows the Home screen.
						- Contains a menu button, product images, and a cart icon.
						- Communicates the layout without implementing navigation.
					- **Lo-fi prototype:**
						- Clicking the menu button opens the Menu screen.
						- Clicking a product allows the user to proceed to the next relevant screen.
						- Clicking the cart opens the Cart screen.
						- The user can follow the flow toward checkout and order confirmation.
					- **Remember:** A lo-fi prototype is built from wireframes, with connections and interactions added to simulate the user experience.
			- **Checklist: What to Check When Building a Lo-Fi Prototype**
				- Use this checklist when creating and reviewing your prototype in Figma.
				- **A. Before Prototyping: Prepare the Screens**
					- ☐ **Review the user flow:** Understand the main task users need to complete.
					- ☐ **Identify required screens:** Ensure all screens needed for the primary user journey exist.
					- ☐ **Create digital wireframes:** Use basic shapes, lines, and text to represent the interface.
					- ☐ **Name each screen clearly:** For example, `Home`, `Menu`, `Cart`, `Checkout`, and `Confirmation`.
					- ☐ **Arrange screens logically:** Organize frames to make the user flow easy to understand and connect.
				- **B. During Prototyping: Connect the Interactions**
					- ☐ **Connect every relevant interactive element:** Buttons and other interactive elements should lead to the appropriate destination or outcome.
					- ☐ **Check destination screens:** Confirm that each connection leads to the intended screen.
					- ☐ **Follow the main user flow:** Connect screens in the order users are expected to complete their tasks.
					- ☐ **Provide backward navigation where appropriate:** Users should be able to return to previous screens when needed.
					- ☐ **Make interactive elements recognizable:** Buttons should look like buttons, not static headings or labels.
					- ☐ **Resolve missing destinations:** If an interactive element has no destination, create the necessary screen or remove the element if it isn't needed.
				- **C. After Prototyping: Test the User Journey**
					- Use Figma's **Play button** to preview the prototype and test it as a user would.
						- 1. Connections
							- ☐ Can users reach every screen required for the main user flow?
							- ☐ Does each interactive element lead to the correct destination?
							- ☐ Are there any buttons that do nothing when clicked?
						- 2. Navigation
							- ☐ Can users move forward through the intended flow?
							- ☐ Can users move backward where appropriate?
							- ☐ Are navigation cues clear?
							- ☐ Does each interaction produce the expected result?
						- 3. Completion
							- ☐ Can users complete the intended task from beginning to end?
							- ☐ Is there a clear indication of successful completion?
							- ☐ Can users return to an appropriate starting point after completing the task?
						- 4. Mobile Preview
							- ☐ Does the design display appropriately on the intended mobile device?
							- ☐ Are the screen layout and interactive elements easy to understand when previewed on mobile?
							- You can use the Figma mobile app to preview designs and prototype interactions on a mobile device.
		- ### Understand ethical and inclusive design
			- **What is bias?**
				- Bias is an unfair or unreasonable judgment that can influence UX research, design decisions, and product inclusivity. Cognitive bias occurs when context influences judgment and decision-making.
			- **Common types of cognitive bias**
				- **Confirmation bias:** Favoring evidence that supports existing beliefs.
				- **Cultural bias:** Interpreting behavior through your own cultural norms.
				- **False-consensus bias:** Assuming others share your opinions.
				- **Framing bias:** Being influenced by how information is presented.
				- **Implicit bias:** Unconscious attitudes and stereotypes.
				- **Social desirability bias:** Giving answers intended to create a favorable impression.
				- **Sunk-cost bias:** Continuing with a decision because of previous investment.
				- **Survivorship bias:** Focusing on successes while overlooking failures.
				- Other types include ambiguity effect, clustering illusion, fundamental attribution, hindsight, racial, serial position, and transparency bias.
			- **Product failures caused by bias**
				- **Airport scanners:** May disadvantage people with certain body characteristics, skin tones, or religious head coverings.
				- **Real-name systems:** May create difficulties for transgender users.
				- **Cars and seat belts:** Historically relied on male-based crash-test models.
				- **Automatic soap dispensers:** Some sensors fail to detect darker skin reliably.
				- These examples show how non-inclusive research and design assumptions can exclude users.
			- **How to reduce bias**
				- Learn to recognize different types of bias.
				- Practice self-awareness and consider other perspectives.
				- Identify and challenge biases in teammates and stakeholders.
				- Treat design ideas as hypotheses and adapt to research findings.
				- Conduct a **pre-mortem** to anticipate potential failures and prevent them early.
				- Working with diverse teams and research participants also helps identify overlooked problems.
			- **Key Takeaway**
				- UX designers cannot eliminate all bias, but they can recognize and reduce it through self-awareness, inclusive research, evidence-based decisions, and willingness to revise designs.
			- ---
			- **What Are Deceptive Patterns?**
				- **Deceptive patterns** are design techniques that trick users into taking actions they did not intend, such as making unwanted purchases or subscriptions. They can involve visual, interactive, audio, or motion elements.
				- The term *deceptive patterns* is often used instead of *dark patterns* to focus on the misleading tactic itself.
			- **Six Common Deceptive Patterns**
			  | Pattern | Definition | How to Avoid It |
			  | ---- | ---- | ---- |
			  | **Forced Continuity** | Charging users after a free trial without warning or reminders. | Notify users before charging and make cancellation easy. |
			  | **Sneak into Basket** | Adding extra items or services to a user's cart without clear consent. | Never preselect items that add purchases to the cart. |
			  | **Hidden Costs** | Revealing additional fees only at the end of checkout. | Display all costs, including shipping and taxes, upfront. |
			  | **Confirmshaming** | Using guilt-inducing language to discourage users from opting out. | Use neutral, respectful language on buttons and confirmation screens. |
			  | **Urgency** | Pressuring users to purchase quickly through time-limited offers. | Avoid using time pressure to manipulate purchasing decisions. |
			  | **Scarcity** | Emphasizing limited stock to encourage immediate purchases. | Communicate availability honestly to inform, not manipulate, users. |
			- **How UX Designers Can Avoid Deceptive Patterns**
				- **Be transparent:** Clearly communicate pricing, subscriptions, and conditions.
				- **Respect user choices:** Make opting out and canceling straightforward.
				- **Use clear language:** Ensure buttons and actions communicate their actual consequences.
				- **Avoid manipulation:** Do not exploit guilt, urgency, or fear of missing out.
				- **Build trust:** Prioritize users' interests over pressuring them into purchases.
			- **Key Takeaway**
				- **Good UX design helps users make informed decisions rather than manipulating them.** Honest communication, clear choices, and transparent processes build user trust and prevent deceptive experiences.
			- ---
			- **What Is the Attention Economy?**
				- The **attention economy** refers to competition for users’ limited attention. When users focus on one thing, they may ignore something else.
				- Psychologist Herbert A. Simon highlighted that human attention and information-processing capacity are limited. Excessive distractions can make it harder for users to complete tasks effectively.

- # 4. Conduct UX Research and Test Early Concepts
	- ## Module 1: Planning UX research studies
		- ### Understand the UX research process
			- **Four  Steps  of  a  UX  Research  Study**
			  ![image.png](../assets/image_1793774800529_0.png)
				- **1. Plan the Study**
					- Define project background and research goals.
					- Prepare research questions and study procedures.
					- Select participants.
				- **2. Conduct the Research**
					- Collect user data using methods such as usability studies.
					- Observe user interactions and identify pain points.
				- **3. Analyze and Synthesize Results**
					- Analyze quantitative data to identify patterns.
					- Analyze qualitative responses to identify trends.
					- Synthesize findings to understand user problems and design opportunities.
				- **4. Share and Promote Insights**
					- Present findings to stakeholders.
					- Include research methodology, data, conclusions, and recommendations.
					- Help stakeholders understand findings and decide on appropriate actions.
			- **Importance  of  UX  Research  Studies**
				- Prioritize user needs: Discover real user problems before product development.
				- Validate assumptions: Bridge the gap between business assumptions and actual user needs.
				- Save resources: Reduce the risk of developing solutions that do not address user problems.
			- **Key  Takeaway**
				- A UX research study follows four steps: Plan → Conduct → Analyze & Synthesize → Share Insights. Its purpose is to make design decisions based on real user needs rather than assumptions.
			- ---
			- **Research  in  the  Test  Phase**
				- Research continues throughout the UX design process, including the final testing phase.
				- Acceptance testing evaluates how real users interact with and respond to the final product before launch. Feedback can still lead to design iterations, usually minor refinements.
			- **Benefits  of  Test  Phase  Research**
				- Identify usability problems before launch.
				- Refine designs based on real user feedback.
				- Reduce development costs by identifying issues during prototype testing.
				- Improve the final user experience.
			- **Types  of  Test  Phase  Research**
			  | Testing Type | Purpose |
			  | User Acceptance Testing (UAT) | Determines whether the product does what it was designed to do. |
			  | Quality Assurance (QA) | Checks for errors and verifies that product features work as intended. Issues can be reported to developers through bug reports. |
			  | Accessibility Evaluation | Ensures the product meets accessibility standards. |
			- **Research  Structure  in  the  Test  Phase**
				- **Test participants**
					- Recruit participants who match the previously defined target-user demographics.
					- Use testing services or recruit participants directly.
				- **Test location**
					- In-person testing: Observe users navigating the product in a lab.
					- Online testing: Record user actions for later review.
				- **Questionnaires**
					- Ask participants to complete specific tasks, followed by questions about their experience.
					- Examples:
						- Can you find a product in your size?
						- How was the checkout experience?
						- Was navigation easy?
						- Did you find all the information you needed?
						- What did you like or dislike?
						- What would you improve?
		- ### Build a UX research plan (part 1)
			- **Seven  Elements  of  a  UX  Research  Plan**
			  | Element | Purpose |
			  | 1. Project Background | Explains the project context and why the research is needed. |
			  | 2. Research Goals | Defines the design problems to solve and how findings will influence design decisions. |
			  | 3. Research Questions | Specifies what the research aims to answer. |
			  | 4. Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) | Defines metrics to measure progress toward research goals, such as task completion rate. |
			  | 5. Methodology | Describes how data will be collected and analyzed, such as through surveys and spreadsheets. |
			  | 6. Participants | Defines the target participants and ensures they represent the intended user group without sample bias. |
			  | 7. Script | Contains the questions or discussion guide used during the research. |
			  ![image.png](../assets/image_1793420300249_0.png)
			- **UX research plan template**
				- https://docs.google.com/document/d/1toX54uaOnCQPPjAb1-a7QPRzpgg1aAt6oezOEjnyjQQ/template/preview

			- **[1|2|3] First Three Key  Elements  of  a  UX  Research  Plan**
				- **1.  Project  Background**
					- Explains the situation and why the research is necessary. Its purpose is to ensure the team shares the same understanding before starting research.
					- Include:
						- Signals indicating the need for research.
						- The problem you want to solve.
						- Previous research and attempted solutions.
						- Expected insights and how they will inform decisions.
					- Keep it concise so the entire team can understand it.
				- **2.  Research  Goals**
					- Define what you want to learn or achieve through research. Research goals guide the entire study.
					- There are three types based on the product lifecycle:
					  | Research Type | Purpose | Dog Walking App Example |
					  | Foundational Research | Understand why or whether a product should be built. | Determine whether users need a dog-walking app. |
					  | Design Research | Understand how to build the product. | Identify necessary features to solve users' problems. |
					  | Post-launch Research | Evaluate whether the launched product works as expected. | Determine whether the app attracts and retains regular users. |
				- **3.  Research  Questions**
					- Define the specific questions that the research aims to answer.
					- Important: Research questions are not the literal questions asked to participants. Those belong in the interview or research script.
					- Good research questions should be:
						- **Actionable**: Clearly indicate what information is needed and how to find it.
						- **Specific**: Focused enough to produce meaningful data.
						- **Neutral**: Avoid assumptions or leading participants toward particular answers.
						- **Methodological**: Indicate whether quantitative or qualitative data is needed.
					- | Research Type | Focus | Example |
					  | Quantitative | Numbers, counting, and measurement. | How many times per week do users hire a dog walker? |
					  | Qualitative | Understanding why and how things happen. | What frustrates users when finding a dog walker? |
				- Both methods can be used together to understand different aspects of the user experience.
			- **Practical  Example:  CoffeeHouse  Research  Plan**
				- The example focuses on testing a low-fidelity prototype for a CoffeeHouse ordering app.
				- **Introduction**
				  | Element | Example |
				  | Title | Creating CoffeeHouse Ordering App |
				  | Author | Ali, UX Researcher |
				  | Stakeholders | CoffeeHouse customers, CTO, VP of Design |
				  | Date | December 14, 2020 |
				  | Project Background | Customers sometimes place group orders. Ordering individually takes too long. The app aims to support collaborative ordering, skip in-store lines, and streamline payment. |
				  | Research Goals | Determine whether collaborative ordering actually saves time when placing group orders. |
				- **Research  Questions**
					- How long does it take for 4–5 people to make a collaborative group order?
					- What can we learn from the steps users take to order as a group versus individually?
					- Why are these good questions?
						- **Actionable**: Researchers know what information to collect.
						- **Specific**: The first question identifies a group size and measurable outcome.
						- **Neutral**: Neither question assumes collaborative ordering is better or worse.
						- **Methodological**: The first produces quantitative data (time), while the second produces qualitative insights (user behavior and experience).
				- **Research  Plan  Assessment  Checklist**
					- Before finalizing your research plan, check the following:
						- **Introduction**
							- ☐ Title clearly represents the study's focus and purpose.
							- ☐ Author's name, title, and contact information are included.
							- ☐ Stakeholders are identified.
							- ☐ Date reflects the latest update.
							- ☐ Project background explains the reason for research.
							- ☐ Research goals clearly describe what you want to learn.
						- **Research Questions**
							- ☐ Actionable and answerable.
							- ☐ Specific enough to produce meaningful data.
							- ☐ Neutral and not leading.
							- ☐ Clear about whether quantitative or qualitative data is needed.
				- **Key  Takeaway**
					- A UX research plan starts with three foundational questions:
						- Project Background: Why are we conducting this research?
						- Research Goals: What do we want to learn?
						- Research Questions: What specific questions must we answer?
				- The project background establishes context, research goals define direction, and research questions determine what data needs to be collected. Together, they ensure research produces meaningful insights that support design decisions.
			- **[4] KPIs**
				- **Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)** are measurable indicators used to evaluate progress toward research goals and determine what works well or needs improvement in a UX design.
				- **Seven  KPIs  in  UX  Research**
				  | KPI | What it measures | How to interpret it |
				  | Time on task | Time users need to complete a task. | Generally, shorter time indicates a more efficient design. |
				  | Use of navigation vs. search | How often users use navigation compared to search. | Reveals user preferences, not necessarily good or bad performance. |
				  | User error rates | How often users make mistakes. | Fewer errors generally indicate a better design. |
				  | Drop-off rates | Number of users who abandon a task before completion. | Lower rates are generally better. |
				  | Conversion rates | Percentage of users who successfully complete a desired action. | Higher rates generally indicate better performance. |
				  | System Usability Scale (SUS) | Perceived usability, measured through a 10-statement questionnaire. | Users rate statements from strongly disagree to strongly agree. |
				  | Net Promoter Score (NPS) | Likelihood that users recommend the product to others. | Calculated as % Promoters − % Detractors. |
				- NPS categories:
					- Promoters: scores 9–10.
					- Passives: scores 7–8.
					- Detractors: scores 0–6.
				- **Choosing  the  Right  KPIs**
					- KPI selection depends on the research goals and the insights you want to obtain. A good KPI should:
						- Measure progress toward a research goal.
						- Reveal meaningful insights into user behavior.
						- Provide actionable feedback about design performance.
					- KPIs can also be combined to reveal deeper problems.
			- **Example  Research  Plan:  CoffeeHouse  KPIs**
				- Project: Testing a low-fidelity prototype for a CoffeeHouse ordering app.
				- Research goal: Determine whether collaborative ordering saves time when placing group orders.
				- Research questions:
					- How long does it take 4–5 people to make a collaborative group order?
					- What can we learn by comparing the steps users take when ordering as a group versus individually?
				- Selected  KPIs
				  | KPI | What it evaluates |
				  | Time on task | Whether group ordering is faster than individual ordering. |
				  | User error rates | How often users get lost or make mistakes during ordering. |
				  | Conversion rates | Whether users successfully complete group orders. |
				- **Combining  KPI  results**
					- For example, if research finds:
						- High time on task
						- High user error rates
						- Low conversion rates
					- This suggests that the group-ordering process is difficult and inefficient. Users may become frustrated and abandon the task.
					- Combining KPIs helps identify problems more accurately than examining each metric independently.
				- **Key  Takeaway**
					- KPIs are accountability tools that help UX designers evaluate whether a design addresses the original problem. Choose KPIs based on research goals, and combine their findings when useful to gain a more complete understanding of user experience.
		- ### Build a UX research plan (part 2)
			- **[5] Methodology**
				- **Methodology** is the set of steps used to conduct research, collect data, and analyze data.
				- When defining a research methodology, consider three things:
					- **Research method**: What method will be used?
					- **Research steps**: What activities are involved?
					- **Data type**: What kind of data will be generated?
				- Comparing  Research  Methods
					- Research methods can be categorized in two ways:
						- Based on the source:
							- **Primary research**: Research conducted directly by you, such as interviews, surveys, competitive audits, and usability studies.
							- **Secondary research**: Research using information collected or prepared by others.
						- Based on the data:
							- **Qualitative research**: Explores why and how things happen through observations and descriptions.
							- **Quantitative research**: Measures or counts information to produce numerical data.
						- Primary qualitative research is commonly used during the Empathize phase to understand users' needs, challenges, and characteristics.
				- **Usability  Study  as  a  Research  Method**
					- For Course 4, the research method is a usability study, which tests a design with real potential users.
					- A usability study is a primary, qualitative research method because designers directly observe participants' behavior and listen to their feedback.
					- Two forms of observation:
						- Direct observation: Give participants guided tasks and take notes on how successfully they complete them.
						- Indirect observation: Record the study and review it later to discover additional insights.
				- **Key  Takeaway**
					- Methodology explains how research will be conducted. It defines the research method, its steps, and the type of data collected. For Course 4, usability studies help evaluate low-fidelity prototypes through direct and recorded observations of real users.
			- **[6] Participants**
				- **UX research participants** should represent the product’s potential users. Their feedback helps designers identify pain points and improve designs through iteration.
				- **Where to Find Participants**
				  | Recruitment method | Description | Considerations |
				  | Existing user base | Recruit people who already use the company's product. | Useful when a company has established users. |
				  | Online | Recruit through social media, design communities, or research platforms such as UserTesting and User Interviews. | May exclude people without internet access or those who are less active online. |
				  | Hallway testing | Ask people nearby to try the product, ideally in locations where the target audience gathers. | Quick and inexpensive, but participants may not represent the target users. |
					- Incentives encourage participation. Examples include money, gift cards, physical gifts, or paid meals.
				- **Selecting the Right Participants**
					- Use a screener survey to determine whether potential participants meet the study requirements. https://www.userinterviews.com/ux-research-field-guide-chapter/screener-surveys
					- Important concepts:
						- Representative sample: A small group representing the target users, including groups that are often marginalized.
						- Marginalized population: People whose characteristics or life experiences prevent them from fitting society's inaccurate definition of "normal."
					- Diversity matters because participants have different backgrounds, perspectives, and abilities. Their feedback helps make products more accessible and equitable.
				- **Inclusive Recruitment and Accessibility**
					- Instead of directly asking about someone's disability status, recruit participants based on the assistive technologies they use.
					- Examples include people who use:
						- Screen readers
						- Closed captions
						- Switch devices
						- Keyboard-only navigation
						- Magnification devices or magnified screens
					- This approach focuses on how people interact with technology rather than defining them by a disability.
				- **Recommended Sample Size**
					- 5–8 participants
					- The lesson recommends recruiting between five and eight participants for a usability study:
					- Five participants can provide valuable feedback.
					- More than eight may produce diminishing returns on investment.
				- **Key Takeaway**
					- Recruit participants who represent your target users and reflect diverse backgrounds, perspectives, and abilities. Use screener surveys to check suitability, consider accessibility needs, and aim for 5–8 participants in a usability study.
			- **Example Research Plan: Methodology and Participants**
				- This lesson explains how to document methodology and participants in a UX research plan. Both sections help stakeholders understand the study and ensure its results are reliable and useful.
				- **1. Methodology Section**
					- Methodology describes how the research will be conducted, including:
						- Study type: For example, moderated or unmoderated usability study.
						- Location: Where the study takes place, including remote or in-person.
						- Date: When research sessions will happen.
						- Participants and procedures: Number of participants and tasks they will complete.
						- Session structure: Activities and estimated duration.
					- **CoffeeHouse example**
					  | Element | Description |
					  | Study type | Unmoderated usability study |
					  | Location | Remote, participants' homes in the US |
					  | Date | February 8–9 |
					  | Participants | Five people complete collaborative ordering tasks independently; one is randomly selected to submit the group order. |
					  | Session duration | 45 minutes |
					  | Session structure | Introduction, tasks, and questionnaire |
					- An unmoderated study means participants complete the instructions, tasks, and questions independently without a moderator.
					- A detailed methodology also makes it easier for other researchers to replicate the study in the future.
				- **2. Participants Section**
					- Describe the characteristics of ideal participants based on research goals and target users.
					- **CoffeeHouse example**
						- People who place group coffee orders at least twice a month.
						- Orders may be for office meetings, friends, or family.
						- Participants do not have to drink coffee themselves.
						- Gender: 2 male, 2 female, and 1 non-binary.
						- Age: 20–75 years old.
						- Accessibility: 1 participant uses assistive technology (keyboard or screen reader).
						- Incentive: $10 CoffeeHouse gift card.
					- The plan should also include a way to thank participants for their time, such as a thank-you message.
				- **3. Research Plan Assessment**
					- Before finalizing these sections, check the following:
					- Methodology
						- Are research procedures clearly described?
						- Are the time and location specified?
						- Is the researcher identified?
						- Is it clear whether the study is moderated or unmoderated?
						- Are the session activities and process explained?
					- Participants
						- Are the participant characteristics relevant to the research goals?
						- Does the sample include diverse perspectives and abilities?
						- Is there an appropriate participation incentive?
						- Is there a way to thank participants?
				- **Key Takeaway**
					- A research plan needs two important details:
						- Methodology: Explains how, when, where, and by whom the study will be conducted.
						- Participants: Explains who will participate, why they are suitable, and how their diversity and accessibility needs are considered.
			- **[7] Script**
				- **A script**, also called a discussion guide, is a list of tasks and follow-up questions presented to participants during a usability study.
				- Its purpose is to understand what users do, think, feel, and experience while interacting with a product.
				- The script should be based on the research questions and KPIs defined earlier in the research plan.
				- **1. Structure of a Research Script**
					- A good script consists of two main parts:
						- **A. Introduction**
							- The introduction should:
								- Ask for participant consent to record audio and video.
								- Explain that the study is not a test and there are no wrong answers.
								- Encourage participants to ask questions.
								- Explain why their data is being collected.
								- Ask basic questions about their background and relationship with the product.
							- These elements help achieve three objectives:
								- Gather demographic information.
								- Establish a professional tone.
								- Make participants feel comfortable.
						- **B. Prompts and Follow-up Questions**
							- Each prompt describes a task for participants to complete. Follow-up questions explore their experience after completing the task.
							- CoffeeHouse example:
							  | Prompt | Task | Follow-up |
							  | 1 | Customize a drink order. | How easy was customization? What was challenging? |
							  | 2 | Start a new group order. | Did you find anything confusing? |
							  | 3 | Add multiple customized drinks to a group order and proceed to checkout. | What was easy or challenging? |
							  | 4 | Complete the group order. | How was paying for multiple orders? How long did it take? |
							  | 5 | Evaluate the app overall. | What did you like and dislike? |
				- **2. Characteristics of Good Prompts**
					- Prompts and follow-up questions should have three characteristics:
						- Open-ended: Encourage participants to explain, act, and reflect instead of answering only yes or no.
						- Objective: Avoid leading participants, providing clues, or influencing their answers.
						- Goal-oriented: Directly relate to research goals and KPIs.
					- This helps researchers collect meaningful feedback and actionable insights.
				- **3. Script Assessment Checklist**
					- Before using a script, check whether it:
						- Starts with an introduction that gathers basic participant information and establishes a comfortable environment.
						- Uses consistent prompts and questions for all participants.
						- Encourages open-ended discussion and reflection.
						- Avoids leading questions and researcher bias.
						- Addresses the research goals and selected KPIs.
				- **Key Takeaway**
					- A research script provides a consistent structure for usability studies. It combines an introduction, participant tasks, and follow-up questions to uncover user behavior, needs, and motivations.
					- Without a well-designed script, design decisions risk being based on assumptions rather than actual user feedback.

	- ## Module 2: Conducting research with usability studies
		- ### Conduct a usability study
			- A **usability study** (also called usability testing) evaluates how easily participants complete core tasks in a design. It helps UX teams understand real user interactions and identify improvements. Studies can be conducted at any design stage, from low-fidelity prototypes to finished products.
			- **1. Types of Usability Studies**
			  | Moderated | Unmoderated |
			  | A moderator guides participants in real time. | Participants complete tasks without live guidance. |
			  | Allows live follow-up questions and clarification. | Often recorded for later review. |
			  | Useful for complex tasks and early prototypes. | Useful for simple tasks and natural interactions. |
			- **2. Advantages and Disadvantages**
				- **Moderated studies**
					- Advantages:
						- Build rapport and encourage detailed feedback.
						- Observe interactions firsthand.
						- Provide live guidance and clarify questions.
						- Explore complex or ambiguous user journeys.
						- Keep participants focused.
					- Disadvantages:
						- Moderator influence or bias.
						- Participants may feel uncomfortable with the moderator.
						- Scheduling can be difficult.
						- Limited availability may exclude some participants.
						- Requires reliable internet for remote sessions.
						- More expensive due to moderator time and possible venue costs.
				- **Unmoderated studies**
					- Advantages:
						- More natural user interactions.
						- Easier to recruit participants across different schedules and time zones.
						- Flexible scheduling.
						- Less expensive.
						- More privacy for sensitive topics.
						- Suitable for simple user journeys.
					- Disadvantages:
						- No real-time support or follow-up questions.
						- Participants may become distracted.
						- Potential intellectual property/privacy risks.
						- Testing platforms may have accessibility limitations.
						- Can generate more irrelevant data to analyze.
						- Not suitable for complex user journeys.
			- **3. What Both Studies Have in Common**
				- Regardless of the type:
					- **Tasks**: Must reflect actions real users would perform in the product.
					- **Script**: Use the same script and task order for every participant to maintain consistency.
					- **Participants**: Should represent the product's target users and align with user personas.
				- For participant numbers, the lesson recommends 5–8 participants: at least five to gather useful feedback, while adding more than eight often has diminishing returns.
			- **Key Takeaway**
			- Choose the study type based on your research goals:
				- **Moderated**: Best when you need detailed feedback, clarification, or guidance through complex tasks.
				- **Unmoderated**: Best when you want natural interactions, flexible scheduling, and lower costs for simple tasks.
				- Both methods help identify usability problems and improve the product.
